From the stool problem to integral_to_quilt_v4.py

Good around the left/right tiles, bad around the middle tiles

The middle tiles correspond to direction dx ≈ D/2 — all hogels looking straight ahead (θ ≈ 0). The left/right tiles correspond to extreme angles (θ ≈ ±θ_max).

The assembled view at the center direction (all hogels looking straight ahead simultaneously) is essentially an orthographic projection — every hogel contributes the pixel directly in front of it. In orthographic projection, near and far edges of the stool appear at their physical width, which for a rectangular stool means near = far. No foreshortening. So near doesn't appear wider than far — and the observer perceives this as near appearing "shorter."

The above definition of "foreshortening" was provided by The Web Art Academy in January 2011. Eric Shaffer, professor at University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign, used this definition in the lecture in Spring 2018 without citing or referring to The Web Art Academy. As seen in the screenshot above, the slide for the lecture asks directly whether foreshortening can happen in orthographic projection. A later slide then lists, under "Orthographic Projection," both "Foreshortens" and "No change in size by depth."

At extreme angles, the 3D shape of the stool itself creates perspective-like cues — you see the side panels, legs become prominent, the near face becomes larger naturally from the scene geometry. So those tiles accidentally look more correct.

The fundamental issue is this:

Having generated a light field image, the necec2021 paper needed to develop a method to view the generated images. This began with generating 2D images from the light field based on orthographic projections of the light field (taking a single pixel from each hogel all corresponding the same light ray angle).
Saad's Python code follows this method. The Python code is not publicly available and I am grateful to Matthew Hamilton (one of the authors of the paper) for giving me access to it. The code clarifies that although in the necec2021 paper the word "parallel" does not even exist, by "orthographic projections" they implied "parallel projections"; and it is not essentially a mistake in choice of words — there are lectures and academic materials that use "orthographic projection" and "parallel projection" synonymously.

The Python code does a light field assembly — one pixel per hogel at a fixed direction. This produces an orthographic view at center direction.

In this project I allow generation of both parallel and perspective views. Reason for including perspective views: According to the nvidia paper light field displays require the generation of multiple perspective views to reconstruct the full light field, necessitating a dense array of rays projected at precise angles. For that I needed to develop a Unity-inspired camera that can cast a ray and fetch information about what the ray hits. This project uses ray tracing technology which involves the ability to cast a ray (a line) from a starting point in a space into a direction.

This requires knowing the camera distance and light field attributes — parameters not in the original script at all. That's why it can't be fixed with a simple line change.

"lightFieldAttributes": { "spatialResolution": [819, 455], "directionalResolution": [81, 1], "displayFOV": [60, 1], "file": "Test.png" }

This gives everything needed. Extracting the key numbers:

  • spatialResolution: [819, 1] — 819 hogels
  • directionalResolution: [81, 1] — 81 views, HPO confirmed
  • displayFOV: [60, 1] → maxAngle = tan(30°) = 0.57735

The formula I derived earlier becomes concrete.

The key change from Saad's version: instead of dx = D-v for every hogel, each hogel gets its own directional index.

python integral_to_quilt_v4.py --config your_config.json --image_path Test.png

The --z_cam_hogels parameter lets you tune perspective strength. The default makes the tan(cameraFOV)=0.5*tan(displayFOV). A smaller value gives stronger perspective; a larger value approaches the flat orthographic output of v2.
